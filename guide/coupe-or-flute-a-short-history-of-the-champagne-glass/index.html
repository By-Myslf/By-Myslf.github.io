Coupe or flute? A short history of the champagne glass

The shallow coupe ruled the 19th century, the flute took over after the war, and today many producers prefer something in between. How to tell what you are looking at.

By Guest Author, Glass historian. Published 12 October 2026

Few objects change shape as obviously with fashion as the champagne glass. Knowing the main shapes, and roughly when each was popular, is the first step to dating a glass.

The coupe

The broad, shallow coupe was the standard champagne glass through most of the 19th century and well into the 20th. A popular legend says it was modelled on the breast of Marie Antoinette, but there is no evidence for this, and similar glasses existed long before her.

Coupes were made in every quality, from pressed glass to finely cut crystal. Look at the stem and foot: hand-finished examples usually show a slightly uneven foot and a ground or polished mark underneath where the glass was broken off the blowpipe.

The flute

The tall, narrow flute keeps the bubbles longer and concentrates the aroma. It became the usual choice from the middle of the 20th century, helped by restaurants and by the producers themselves.

Engraved and etched flutes from the 1920s and 1930s are especially collectable. Check the rim for tiny chips, which are easy to miss and hard to repair.

The tulip

Many champagne houses now recommend a glass with a wider bowl that narrows at the top. It is a modern shape, so a tulip glass is unlikely to be older than the late 20th century.

Quick checks for dating

  • Weight and ring: lead crystal feels heavier and rings clearly when tapped gently.
  • Mould seams: fine lines down the side suggest a pressed or mould-blown glass.
  • Wear on the foot: a century of use leaves fine scratches in a ring under the foot. Perfectly clean feet on an “old” glass deserve a second look.
