Champagne Salon – The History of a Champagne House from 1905 to the Present Day

Eugène-Aimé Salon’s vision, Chardonnay from Le Mesnil-sur-Oger, the story of stolen Champagne, Bernard de Nonancourt and the Laurent-Perrier Group – more than 120 years of Champagne history.

A Champagne That Challenged Tradition

The history of Champagne Salon began in 1905, when Eugène-Aimé Salon created a Champagne that was fundamentally different from the established practices of the time.

At the beginning of the 20th century, the major Champagne houses generally produced their wines by blending different grape varieties, vineyards and vintages. The purpose of this practice was to maintain a consistent house style despite variations in growing conditions from year to year.

Eugène-Aimé Salon, however, wanted to build his Champagne around a single grape variety, vineyards from a single village and the harvest of a single vintage.

He believed that Chardonnay from Le Mesnil-sur-Oger, in the Côte des Blancs, was capable of producing an exceptional, complex and long-lived Champagne on its own.

The first Salon vintage was produced in 1905. Initially, it was not intended for commercial sale but for the founder’s personal consumption and for his friends and guests.

Champagne Salon subsequently became one of the most important historical pioneers of Blanc de Blancs Champagne made exclusively from Chardonnay.

The house continues to follow the fundamental principles of its founder’s original vision: it produces just one vintage Champagne, made exclusively from Chardonnay grown in Le Mesnil-sur-Oger, and only when the quality of the harvest meets its demanding standards.

Sources: Champagne Salon official history [1]; Decanter [11].

Eugène-Aimé Salon – From the Fur Trade to Champagne

Eugène-Aimé Salon was born in 1867 in Pocancy, in the Champagne region of France.

His family was involved in agriculture, but as a young man he moved to Paris, where he began working in the fur industry.

He built his career at Chapal, a fur manufacturing and trading company. Thanks to his business success and international connections, he gradually became a well-known figure in Parisian society.

He travelled regularly, developed an interest in gastronomy and particularly appreciated fine wines.

Although he lived in Paris, he maintained close ties with Champagne. When he decided to create his own Champagne, he sought the assistance of his brother-in-law, Marcel Guillaume.

Guillaume was an experienced cellar master who knew the vineyards of the Côte des Blancs exceptionally well. Together, they investigated which sites were best suited to producing Chardonnay Champagne intended for extended ageing.

They eventually selected Le Mesnil-sur-Oger.

Salon’s concept was unusual because many winemakers at the time believed that Pinot Noir was better suited to long ageing. Chardonnay was often considered too acidic and less capable of producing wines with sufficient structure and longevity.

Salon set out to demonstrate precisely the opposite.

He believed that Chardonnay from the right terroir could develop remarkable complexity, texture and structure through prolonged ageing, without the addition of other grape varieties.

The first vintage, produced in 1905, was followed by further experiments. These early bottles were made in extremely limited quantities and were not commercially available.

In 1911, Salon purchased his own vineyard in Le Mesnil-sur-Oger. This became the vineyard later known as Le Jardin de Salon.

Salon died in 1943.

Sources: Champagne Salon [1]; La Fête du Champagne / Peter Liem [9]; Decanter [11]; Club Oenologique [4].

The First Vintages and Commercial Launch

According to Champagne Salon’s official history, the first vintage was produced in 1905, while the business was formally established in 1920 for commercial purposes.

There are minor discrepancies in the specialist literature concerning the exact chronology of the first commercially released Salon Champagnes.

Peter Liem’s Champagne reference material and several other sources identify the 1921 vintage as the first commercially produced Salon. The official Champagne Salon website places the commercial introduction more generally in the 1920s.

The dates should therefore be distinguished: 1905 marks the first vintage produced, 1920 the formal establishment of the company, and the 1920s the period when the Champagne entered the commercial market.

The Maxim’s Wine List – 1928

One of the most interesting surviving documents from Salon’s early history is a 1928 wine list from the famous Parisian restaurant Maxim’s.

Adam Lechmere discusses this historical document in his article published by Club Oenologique on January 23, 2024.

The wine list features the 1921 Salon vintage under the following description:

Salon & Cie, Mesnil Nature, Cuvée Pure de Raisins Blancs 1921, Vin de Grand Cru (Blanc de Blancs).

This historical designation is significant because it demonstrates that the term “Blanc de Blancs” was not yet as widely used as it is today.

The document also provides evidence of Salon’s presence in prestigious Parisian gastronomy.

According to the historical chronology published by the Union des Maisons de Champagne, Salon became the house Champagne at Maxim’s in 1928, receiving a prominent position on the restaurant’s wine list.

During the interwar years, Maxim’s was one of the best-known restaurants in Paris. Salon’s presence there played an important role in establishing its international reputation.

Sources: Club Oenologique [4]; Union des Maisons de Champagne [3].

Le Mesnil-sur-Oger – The Importance of Terroir

Salon is produced exclusively from grapes grown in Le Mesnil-sur-Oger, a village in the Côte des Blancs.

The village is classified as Grand Cru and is regarded as one of Champagne’s most distinguished Chardonnay-growing locations.

Its characteristic chalky limestone soils play an important role in shaping the structure of the wines.

The deep chalk layers can retain water while also providing effective drainage. This is particularly important during periods of limited rainfall.

Chardonnay from the area is known for its pronounced acidity, citrus and green apple aromas, and a distinctive chalky, often saline character.

These qualities make the wines particularly well suited to extended bottle ageing.

One of Salon’s fundamental principles is that it does not blend grapes from different villages. All of its Chardonnay comes exclusively from Le Mesnil-sur-Oger.

The Twenty Vineyard Parcels

According to Champagne Salon’s official information, the wine currently comes from twenty carefully selected vineyard parcels.

The best known is Le Jardin de Salon, located close to the Champagne house itself.

The estate describes this as an approximately one-hectare vineyard under its direct ownership. A more detailed historical account published by Decanter refers to an original area of 0.77 hectares.

Grapes from the other nineteen parcels are sourced through long-term relationships with contracted growers.

According to Peter Liem’s specialist account, the vineyards supplying Salon cover approximately twelve hectares in total, with a substantial proportion of vineyard work supervised by Salon’s own team.

Le Jardin de Salon was most recently replanted in 2002.

The house’s long-standing relationships with its selected vineyard parcels are particularly important in maintaining a consistent style.

However, the precise composition of the vineyards has not remained entirely unchanged throughout the past century.

Sources: Champagne Salon [1]; La Fête du Champagne / Peter Liem [9]; Decanter [11]; Club Oenologique [4].

The Shared History of Salon and Krug Clos du Mesnil

The history of Salon’s vineyard sources includes one of the most interesting changes in Champagne’s vineyard ownership.

The 1.84-hectare walled Chardonnay vineyard now known as Krug Clos du Mesnil once supplied grapes used in Salon.

The vineyard belonged to the Tarin family, and Eugène-Aimé Salon had purchased grapes from this site since the early years of his Champagne production.

According to the historical account published by Club Oenologique, Clos du Mesnil was among the vineyard parcels originally selected for Salon.

In 1971, the Krug Champagne house purchased Clos du Mesnil.

The acquisition date is also confirmed by Krug’s corporate history.

However, the change in ownership did not necessarily bring an immediate end to Salon’s access to the grapes.

According to Decanter and later specialist accounts involving Krug’s cellar master, fruit from Clos du Mesnil may still have contributed to the 1976 Salon vintage.

Krug began treating the vineyard as the source of its own distinctive single-vineyard Champagne towards the end of the 1970s.

The first Krug Clos du Mesnil was produced from the 1979 vintage and commercially released in 1986.

Today, Krug Clos du Mesnil is one of the house’s most celebrated single-vineyard vintage Champagnes.

The histories of the two houses are therefore connected not only geographically, but also through the earlier use of the same vineyard.

This relationship also qualifies the frequently repeated claim that Salon has used precisely the same twenty vineyard parcels without interruption since 1905.

The continuity of its terroir-focused philosophy remains intact, but the composition of its vineyard sources has evolved over time.

Sources: Club Oenologique [4]; Krug / Moët Hennessy corporate history [8]; Decanter [11]; Word on the Grapevine, 2025 [17].

Champagne Production Techniques

Champagne Salon produces exclusively vintage Blanc de Blancs Champagne.

The wine is made from 100% Chardonnay, and each release comes entirely from a single harvest.

No other grape varieties are used, and reserve wines from previous vintages are not blended into the final wine.

The grapes are harvested at an appropriate stage of ripeness, with particular attention paid to the balance between acidity, sugar levels and aromatic maturity.

After pressing, the highest-quality juice fraction is selected for the base wine.

Fermentation in Stainless Steel

Champagne Salon has used stainless steel tanks for primary fermentation since the early 1980s.

In his interview with Club Oenologique, Didier Depond explained that he and former winemaker Alain Terrier did not consider oak fermentation appropriate for Salon’s style.

In their view, oak could obscure the freshness and purity of aromas they wanted to preserve.

Fermentation in stainless steel allows precise temperature control and helps retain the original aromatic character of Chardonnay.

This also provides an important example of how Salon’s fundamental production philosophy has remained consistent for more than a century, even though its technology has evolved.

Avoiding Malolactic Fermentation

One of the defining technical characteristics of Salon production is that the base wine does not undergo malolactic fermentation.

In many other wines, this process converts part of the sharper malic acid into softer lactic acid, generally producing a rounder perception of acidity.

Salon deliberately preserves the wine’s original acid structure.

The house regards this pronounced, firm acidity as essential to its extended ageing potential and desired style.

Secondary Fermentation in Bottle

The base wine is bottled according to the traditional Champagne method.

A mixture of yeast and sugar, known as liqueur de tirage, is added before the bottle is sealed. During secondary fermentation, the yeast converts the sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide.

Because the bottle is sealed, the carbon dioxide remains dissolved in the wine, creating Champagne’s characteristic bubbles.

Following secondary fermentation, the wine remains in contact with the spent yeast cells, or lees, for an extended period.

Ageing – An Average of Ten Years in the Cellar

According to Champagne Salon’s official information, the wines spend an average of ten years ageing in the cellar.

This does not mean that every vintage is released after exactly ten years.

Some vintages require considerably longer, and the house decides when to disgorge and release each wine according to its development.

During prolonged lees ageing, the gradual breakdown of yeast cells can contribute to increasingly complex aromas, a finer texture and greater depth.

The natural acidity of Chardonnay from Le Mesnil-sur-Oger is a crucial factor in enabling this extended maturation.

At the end of the ageing period, the sediment is collected in the neck of the bottle and removed through disgorgement.

Dosage – A Restrained Addition of Sugar

Salon traditionally uses a relatively low dosage, meaning that only a limited quantity of expedition liqueur is added after disgorgement.

In his Club Oenologique interview, Didier Depond referred to a dosage of 5–7 grams per litre.

This allows the wine to retain its firm acidity while providing sufficient balance.

The resulting Champagne typically displays citrus, white flower and chalky-saline characteristics in its youth. With further ageing, it may develop more complex notes of toast, honey and mature fruit.

Sources: Champagne Salon [1]; Club Oenologique, interview with Didier Depond [4]; Decanter [11].

Production – Few Vintages and Limited Quantities

One of Champagne Salon’s most important characteristics is that it is not produced every year.

The house releases Salon only when the quality of the grapes and the structure of the wine meet its long-term standards.

Only 37 Salon vintages were produced during the 20th century.

This represents an average of just 3.7 vintages per decade.

Eight vintages from the first quarter of the 21st century had been commercially released under the Salon name: 2002, 2004, 2006, 2007, 2008, 2012, 2013 and 2015.

According to the house’s official records, Salon 2015 represents the 45th released vintage.

Production volumes vary considerably between vintages.

According to Club Oenologique and other specialist sources, even a relatively large vintage typically produces no more than approximately 60,000 standard bottles.

This is not a fixed annual production figure, nor does it mean that every vintage reaches this volume.

Actual quantities are frequently much smaller.

The Exceptional 2008 Vintage

Salon 2008 provides one of the clearest examples of how dramatically production can differ from the usual pattern.

The house produced just 8,000 magnum bottles of this vintage.

The wine was bottled exclusively in the 1.5-litre magnum format and released as part of a special collectors’ presentation.

The so-called Salon Le Mesnil Oenothèque Case contained one magnum of Salon 2008, together with two standard bottles each of the 2004, 2006 and 2007 vintages.

The house justified the exclusive use of magnums by referring to the slower, more balanced maturation that larger bottles can provide.

The Different Release Schedules of the 2014 and 2015 Vintages

According to a Decanter specialist report published in 2026, the 2014 Salon vintage required a longer period on the lees, so the house released the 2015 vintage first.

This is an unusual, though not unprecedented, decision in Salon’s history.

The 2012, 2013, 2014 and 2015 vintages together form an exceptional sequence. A comparable series of four consecutive Salon vintages had previously occurred only between 1946 and 1949.

The different ageing periods demonstrate that Salon’s release schedule is not determined simply by the calendar, but by the development of each individual wine.

What Happens to the Grapes in Years When Salon Is Not Produced?

In years when no Salon is made, grapes from the selected vineyards may be used in other Champagnes.

The most important destination is Champagne Delamotte, which operates in the same village and belongs to the Laurent-Perrier Group.

Some of the fruit may be used for Delamotte Champagnes, while other quantities may be incorporated into additional wines within the group.

This arrangement means that Salon is not obliged to release a Champagne under its own name every year.

Sources: Champagne Salon official vintage records [1]; Club Oenologique [4]; Decanter, 2026 [12]; Vinous [18]; La Fête du Champagne [9].

The Second World War – The Stolen Champagne Salon

One of the most fascinating, and frequently misunderstood, chapters in Champagne Salon’s history is connected with the Second World War.

Following the German occupation of France in 1940, Nazi authorities appropriated substantial quantities of French wine and Champagne.

The valuable stocks of Bordeaux, Burgundy and Champagne were particularly attractive to the German military and occupation authorities.

Champagne Salon did not escape these requisitions.

The central figure in the later story was Bernard de Nonancourt, who would become one of Laurent-Perrier’s most influential leaders after the war.

Bernard de Nonancourt’s Military Service

Bernard de Nonancourt was born in 1920.

His mother, Marie-Louise de Nonancourt, purchased the Laurent-Perrier Champagne house in 1939.

After the outbreak of the Second World War, Bernard and his elder brother Maurice became involved in the French Resistance.

Maurice was captured by the Germans and died in the Sachsenhausen-Oranienburg concentration camp.

Bernard survived the war and subsequently served in General Philippe Leclerc’s renowned French 2nd Armoured Division.

According to Laurent-Perrier’s official annual report, he held the rank of sergeant in the 501st Tank Regiment.

In May 1945, his unit arrived in the Berchtesgaden area, where Adolf Hitler maintained his Bavarian residences and several Nazi leadership facilities were located.

The Discovery of the Berchtesgaden Wine Cellar

The story is described in detail on page 5 of Laurent-Perrier’s official 2011–2012 Annual Report.

According to the document, Bernard de Nonancourt was assigned to inventory Adolf Hitler’s private wine cellar in Berchtesgaden because of his origins in Champagne and his knowledge of wine.

Inside the cellar, he discovered several hundred cases of Champagne Salon.

The company’s account states that these included cases whose confiscation he had personally witnessed five years earlier.

The story is supported by other French sources.

Le Monde’s obituary of Bernard de Nonancourt, published on November 13, 2010, mentions that he participated in the inventory of Hitler’s private wine cellar in Berchtesgaden on May 4, 1945.

A historical account published by La Revue du vin de France refers to several hundred cases of 1928 Salon Champagne that were taken in 1940 and rediscovered in 1945.

The historical foundation of this episode is therefore well established.

Nevertheless, the surviving accounts do not agree on every detail, and the precise number of cases and the full circumstances of the discovery cannot be reconstructed with absolute certainty.

Sources: Laurent-Perrier Annual Report 2011–2012, page 5 [2]; Le Monde [13]; La Revue du vin de France [14].

Hitler’s Eagle’s Nest and the Historical Legend

Accounts of the discovery of Salon Champagne frequently identify Hitler’s Eagle’s Nest, known in German as the Kehlsteinhaus, as the location.

The building stands near Berchtesgaden in the Obersalzberg area, at an altitude of 1,834 metres above sea level, or approximately 6,017 feet.

The Kehlsteinhaus was constructed between 1937 and 1938 at the initiative of Martin Bormann.

It was conceived as a prestigious reception building intended to impress senior visitors, diplomats and foreign guests.

A dedicated mountain road, a tunnel excavated into the rock and an elevator were constructed to provide access to the building.

The tunnel was approximately 126 metres long, while the elevator travelled vertically through a height of approximately 124 metres.

The elevator interior was fitted with polished brass panels, mirrors and leather-covered seating.

Mussolini’s Gift

One frequently repeated story claims that Benito Mussolini gave the elevator to Hitler as a present.

This is incorrect.

Mussolini’s gift was the fireplace made of red Carrara marble in the Kehlsteinhaus, not the elevator.

Hitler’s Fiftieth Birthday

The Kehlsteinhaus is often described as a gift presented to Hitler for his fiftieth birthday on April 20, 1939.

However, research by the historical institution Dokumentation Obersalzberg indicates that surviving primary sources do not substantiate this popular claim.

The building had already been completed and handed over in mid-1938. Historians therefore do not consider the birthday-gift story to be firmly established.

Hitler himself rarely used the building. Historical records document approximately fourteen visits.

His actual residence at Obersalzberg was the Berghof, situated further down the mountain.

The 500,000-Bottle Wine Collection

Accounts of the Berchtesgaden wine cellar frequently refer to a collection containing half a million bottles.

According to the American National WWII Museum, French troops discovered a vast stock of wine and Champagne in May 1945, estimated at approximately 500,000 bottles.

The collection included valuable French wines, Champagnes and cognacs.

However, the figure of half a million should be treated as an estimate, since no complete contemporary inventory has been identified that independently verifies every bottle.

Caution is also necessary regarding the precise location.

Some recollections associate the discovery with the Kehlsteinhaus itself, while others refer more generally to Hitler’s private wine cellar in Berchtesgaden.

Laurent-Perrier’s official corporate documentation uses the broader description “private wine cellar in Berchtesgaden.”

It is therefore more historically accurate to state that Bernard de Nonancourt participated in the discovery of stolen French Champagnes in the Berchtesgaden area than to categorically place the collection inside the Eagle’s Nest building at the summit.

The story also does not establish that Adolf Hitler personally appreciated or collected Salon Champagne. The presence of the stolen wines was primarily a consequence of requisitions during the occupation of France.

Sources: Laurent-Perrier [2]; National WWII Museum [15]; Dokumentation Obersalzberg [16]; Kehlsteinhaus historical information [19].

Bernard de Nonancourt’s Return to Champagne

After the war, Bernard de Nonancourt returned to the family business.

His mother insisted that he acquire practical experience in every stage of Champagne production before assuming a managerial position.

Bernard therefore worked in vineyards and cellars, including periods at the Lanson and Delamotte Champagne houses.

According to Laurent-Perrier’s official corporate history, he took charge of the company in 1948, at the age of twenty-eight.

At that time, the Champagne house employed approximately twenty people and sold around 80,000 bottles annually.

Under Bernard de Nonancourt’s leadership, Laurent-Perrier gradually became one of Champagne’s major international producers.

Important developments included temperature-controlled fermentation, broader use of stainless steel tanks and the creation of premium Champagne cuvées.

Laurent-Perrier introduced its Grand Siècle prestige cuvée in 1958, followed by Cuvée Rosé Brut in 1968.

Alongside international expansion, Bernard de Nonancourt placed particular emphasis on quality and long-term brand development.

The predecessor of Laurent-Perrier was established in 1812 in Tours-sur-Marne by André-Michel Pierlot.

This foundation date belongs to Laurent-Perrier, not to Champagne Salon.

Sources: Laurent-Perrier official history [20]; Laurent-Perrier Annual Report [2].

Changes of Ownership at Champagne Salon

Following Eugène-Aimé Salon’s death in 1943, the Champagne house remained in family ownership for a period, but underwent several changes of ownership in subsequent decades.

According to the official chronology of the Union des Maisons de Champagne, in 1963 Marcel Guillaume, the founder’s grandson, was forced to sell the business because of financial difficulties associated with inheritance taxes.

Champagne Salon was acquired by Besserat de Bellefon, which at the time belonged to Compagnie Générale Dubonnet-Cinzano.

In 1976, the Pernod Ricard Group acquired Besserat de Bellefon and A. Salon et Cie.

This period remains one of the lesser-known chapters in Salon’s history.

Although the Champagne already had a distinguished heritage, its commercial position and international recognition were not comparable with those it would acquire in later decades.

When Salon Champagne Was Given Away to Restaurants

Didier Depond, president of Salon and Delamotte since 1997, recalled a surprising detail from the Pernod Ricard era in his 2024 interview with Club Oenologique.

According to Depond, the company offered certain restaurant customers six or twelve bottles of Salon free of charge when they purchased 100 bottles of Besserat de Bellefon Champagne.

As a young salesman, shortly after Salon entered the Laurent-Perrier Group, Depond visited the Hôtel Raphael in Paris.

When he offered Salon to the sommelier, he was told that the hotel was not accustomed to paying for it.

The restaurant had previously received Salon for free as part of its Besserat de Bellefon purchases.

This anecdote illustrates just how dramatically Champagne Salon’s commercial reputation has changed over the decades.

A Champagne now regarded as a highly valuable collectors’ wine was once used as a promotional incentive.

According to Depond, Pernod Ricard had not fully recognised the value of the brand in its portfolio.

Under Laurent-Perrier ownership, Salon’s brand development increasingly emphasised its rarity, strictly limited production, historical heritage and distinctive winemaking philosophy.

Source: Adam Lechmere, Club Oenologique, January 23, 2024 [4].

The 1988 Acquisition – Salon Joins the Laurent-Perrier Group

The next major turning point in Champagne Salon’s history came in 1988.

The Laurent-Perrier Group, led by Bernard de Nonancourt, acquired the Champagne house.

The official chronology of the Union des Maisons de Champagne records September 29, 1988, as the date of the transaction.

Laurent-Perrier’s corporate reports likewise identify 1988 as the year of the Salon acquisition.

Some later specialist articles give 1989, but the precisely dated professional chronology and original corporate documentation should be regarded as the more authoritative sources.

The acquisition also carried a remarkable personal historical connection.

In 1945, Bernard de Nonancourt had participated as a French soldier in the discovery of stolen Salon Champagne.

Forty-three years later, the company under his leadership acquired Champagne Salon.

Some specialist accounts suggest that the wartime experience may have contributed to his personal interest in the brand.

Both events are documented, but the existence of a direct causal motivation cannot be established with complete certainty.

Following the acquisition, Salon retained its own name, production principles and headquarters in Le Mesnil-sur-Oger.

The Laurent-Perrier Group provided new opportunities through its international distribution network and long-term brand development.

Sources: Union des Maisons de Champagne [3]; Laurent-Perrier [2]; Club Oenologique [4].

The Relationship Between Champagne Delamotte and Salon

The history of Salon is closely connected with Champagne Delamotte.

Founded in 1760 by François Delamotte in Reims, Delamotte is one of the oldest Champagne houses.

The house became associated with the de Nonancourt family during the first half of the 20th century.

According to the Union des Maisons de Champagne chronology, Marie-Louise de Nonancourt acquired the Delamotte Père et Fils brand and its building in Le Mesnil-sur-Oger on May 22, 1939.

The house was subsequently managed by her son, Charles de Nonancourt.

Delamotte’s official corporate history highlights 1988 in relation to its integration into the Laurent-Perrier Group, while the Union des Maisons de Champagne dates the formal acquisition within the group to 1989.

The difference probably reflects the distinction between existing family ownership, organisational integration and the legal date of the transaction.

Since then, the operations of Salon and Delamotte have been closely connected.

Both houses are based in Le Mesnil-sur-Oger, and Chardonnay plays a defining role in their production.

However, their winemaking philosophies differ.

Salon is made only in selected vintages, exclusively from Chardonnay, and does not undergo malolactic fermentation.

Delamotte offers a broader range of Champagnes, producing several different styles and generally allowing malolactic fermentation during winemaking.

Both houses have been led by Didier Depond since 1997, while the winemaking operation is overseen by Michel Fauconnet, cellar master of the Laurent-Perrier Group.

Sources: Champagne Delamotte official history [5]; Union des Maisons de Champagne – Delamotte chronology [6]; La Fête du Champagne [9].

The Evolution of Salon’s Labels and Bottles

It is often claimed that Champagne Salon has used the same label and bottle for more than a hundred years.

This is not historically accurate.

The 2024 Club Oenologique historical study documents the evolution of Salon’s visual identity in considerable detail.

The Original 1905 Label

The first label was designed by Eugène-Aimé Salon himself.

Its appearance was restrained, with simple typography and an emphasis on the Salon name.

The distinctive letter S was already present, although its shape and graphic treatment differed from later designs.

This original visual identity remained the basis of Salon’s presentation for more than half a century.

The 1971 Redesign

With the 1971 vintage, Salon introduced a new bottle shape and substantially redesigned its label.

The new graphic identity featured a more prominent, Art Deco-inspired letter S.

According to historical documentation, the 1971 and 1973 vintages were both released in the original and the redesigned bottle formats.

This is particularly important for collectors because genuine, factory-original bottles from the same vintage can exist in different designs.

The Green Label Introduced in 1982

The dark green label associated with Salon today first appeared in 1982.

The presentation of the brand name gradually became simpler, with the standalone name “Salon” becoming more prominent than the earlier “A. Salon” designation.

Later versions adopted the dark green background, stylised gold S and restrained typography that have become defining features of Salon’s modern identity.

The history of the label can therefore be divided into three principal periods: the original presentation used from 1905, the redesigned identity introduced in 1971 and the green-label design introduced in 1982.

When evaluating older Salon bottles, collectors should therefore consider not only the vintage but also the bottle shape and specific label variation.

Source: Club Oenologique, Adam Lechmere, January 23, 2024 [4].

The Historical Bottle Collection

The rarity of Salon is illustrated by the small number of early vintages remaining in the house’s own cellars.

Audrey Campos, Salon’s brand development manager, discussed the historical collection in the Club Oenologique article published in 2024.

According to her statement, the house then held just one bottle of the 1921 vintage, two bottles of 1928 and four bottles of 1943.

No bottles from the 1930s remained in the house’s own collection.

These figures refer to the company’s cellar holdings at the time of the 2024 statement, not to the total number of surviving bottles worldwide.

The historical collection is significant for several reasons.

Older bottles allow the house to study the development of its winemaking style over decades, examine historical bottle shapes and labels, and evaluate the ageing potential of individual vintages.

The exceptionally small number of surviving bottles also plays an important role in collectors’ demand.

Source: Club Oenologique [4].

Didier Depond and Long-Term Planning

Didier Depond has led Champagne Salon and Champagne Delamotte since 1997.

In his Club Oenologique interview, he emphasised that Salon must operate according to an exceptionally long-term perspective.

According to Depond, at the time of the 2024 interview, the cellars already contained vintages intended for release over the following fifteen years.

This approach differs from the shorter commercial cycles typical of many wine businesses.

For Salon, a vintage may not reach the market until more than a decade after the harvest.

Many decisions are therefore made about wines that consumers will not see for many years.

The Impact of Climate Change

Depond also discussed the consequences of climate change in the 2024 interview.

He noted that Chardonnay harvesting previously often began in mid- or late September, whereas in recent years it has frequently started as early as late August.

Warmer weather can accelerate grape ripening, but it also introduces new challenges in vineyard management and plant protection.

Depond specifically mentioned the gradual appearance of Mediterranean-type vegetation and the spread of new insect species.

Changing climatic conditions require growers to pay increasing attention to vine health, water availability and the precise timing of the harvest.

Nevertheless, Depond did not expect Salon to begin releasing substantially more vintages in the coming decades.

He continued to regard strict vintage selection as one of the brand’s defining principles.

Source: Club Oenologique, interview with Didier Depond [4].

Champagne Salon at International Wine Auctions

Older Champagne Salon vintages regularly appear at international wine auctions.

Their collectors’ value is influenced by several factors.

These include the quality of the vintage, original production quantities, the rarity of surviving bottles, storage conditions, bottle size, label and capsule condition, and documented provenance.

Proper storage is especially important for older Salon bottles.

Champagne is a complex and delicate wine, and its condition can be significantly affected by temperature fluctuations over several decades.

Magnum bottles often command particular interest among collectors, partly because the larger format may provide more favourable conditions for slow, balanced maturation.

The Pandolfini Auction of December 11, 2015

One documented example of the collectors’ market for Champagne Salon is the wine auction held by Pandolfini Casa d’Aste in Milan on December 11, 2015.

The event took place at the Centro Svizzero.

A professional tasting and discussion involving the Salon and Delamotte Champagne houses accompanied the auction.

Didier Depond, president of both houses, participated in the event.

The tasting included Salon vintages 2002, 1997 and 1983, as well as Delamotte 1999 and 1970.

Lot 4 was a 1971 Champagne Salon magnum.

This is the precise result documented by the auction house.

It is also important to note that the bottle was not a standard 750 ml format but a 1.5-litre magnum.

The 1971 vintage is particularly interesting to collectors because it coincides with the introduction of Salon’s redesigned bottle and label.

The Pandolfini auction therefore provides both a documented market value and a historical example of demand for older Salon bottles.

Source: Pandolfini Casa d’Aste, official auction results, December 11, 2015 [7].

Champagne Salon’s Position in the Modern Champagne World

Champagne Salon has become one of the most recognised vintage Blanc de Blancs Champagnes produced in limited quantities.

Its distinctive character is not the result of one individual technical procedure, but of a production philosophy in which virtually every vineyard and winemaking decision serves the creation of one particular style.

The house does not maintain an extensive product range, does not produce a non-vintage Champagne for annual release and does not attempt to eliminate differences between vintages.

Its production remains based on Chardonnay from a single Grand Cru village.

The wines undergo extended ageing and are released only when they have reached the appropriate stage of development.

Over more than a century, some of the vineyard sources have changed, technology has evolved, and the labels have been redesigned several times.

Ownership has also changed, from the original family business through the Besserat de Bellefon and Pernod Ricard periods to the Laurent-Perrier Group.

Yet the defining concept has survived.

Salon’s long-term significance lies in its commitment to a consistent terroir-driven and winemaking philosophy, in which production volume remains secondary to the selection of suitable vintages.

Conclusion

The history of Champagne Salon is closely connected with the development of French Champagne production, Parisian gastronomy, the Second World War and the international fine-wine collecting market.

In 1905, Eugène-Aimé Salon created a Champagne intended to express the characteristics of a single terroir, a single grape variety and a single vintage.

The business entered the commercial market during the 1920s and became more widely known through its association with the celebrated Parisian restaurant Maxim’s.

During the Second World War, stocks of Salon Champagne were seized by the German occupiers. Some of these stolen wines were discovered in the Berchtesgaden area in 1945 by French troops, including Bernard de Nonancourt.

In 1988, de Nonancourt, by then head of the Laurent-Perrier Group, acquired Champagne Salon.

Under Laurent-Perrier ownership, the brand gradually strengthened its international position. A Champagne that had once been given away to certain restaurants alongside purchases of other brands developed into a highly valued collectors’ wine.

The fundamental winemaking principles largely survived, although the vineyard sources, production technology and visual identity evolved over time.

The 37 vintages produced during the 20th century, limited bottle quantities, an average ageing period of ten years and strict vineyard and vintage selection have collectively made Salon one of the most distinctive Champagnes in the history of the region.
