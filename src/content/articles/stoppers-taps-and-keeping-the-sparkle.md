Champagne that has been opened loses its bubbles quickly. For well over a century, people have tried to slow that down, and the results are among the most varied objects a champagne collector can find.

## Clamp stoppers

The most common type is a cap with a rubber seal and two hinged arms that hook under the collar of the bottle. They were made in nickel, brass and silver plate, often with the name of a champagne house or a hotel stamped on top. Check that the hinges move freely and that the seal is not perished.

## Champagne taps

A champagne tap is a hollow, pointed tube with a small valve. It was screwed through the cork so that a glass could be poured without opening the bottle. Taps were popular around 1900, and are often said to have been used to serve small glasses to invalids, for whom champagne was a common tonic. Complete examples, still in their original case, are the most desirable.

## Openers and pliers

Tight corks led to special pliers and grips for turning them out safely. Many were given away by champagne houses as advertising, so look for a name on the handle.

## What to look out for

- **Maker's or advertising marks**, which help with dating and add interest.
- **Completeness:** missing seals, screws or cases are common.
- **Later copies:** some popular designs were reproduced. Crisp, unworn edges on a "Victorian" piece can be a warning sign.
